Part I · Proto-Elamite (c. 3100–2900 BCE)

From Archetype to Clay

Locating Proto-Elamite signs on real tablets, using the numerals as anchors, with collation support for the corpus

Thesis in one sentence

Numerals are the best-understood and most visually distinctive part of the script, so we detect them first, use them to cut each tablet face into entry-sized segments, and match the undeciphered signs in each segment to their transliterations. The result is the first image-grounded dataset of Proto-Elamite sign tokens.

Why this and not "decipherment"

One group, Born, Kelley, Monroe and Sarkar, has already published steadily on the machine-learning version of this pitch. Their work covers sign clustering and topic models (2019), embeddings of sign images (2021), header detection (2022), deep clustering of the sign inventory (2023) and numeral disambiguation (2025). The 2025 paper already maps which signs appear with which number system, so "infer meaning from numerals" is taken.

Both agents independently found the same opening, in Born et al. 2023. Their visual models use one idealised Dahl drawing per sign type (1,319 images), and they note the drawings "smooth over many of the irregularities of the original shapes drawn on clay." Nobody has built a sign detector or a token-level image dataset for Proto-Elamite. A method for this exists in cuneiform research: Dencker et al. 2020 trained a detector using only the transliterations as labels.

The key technical fix from review

Both first drafts assumed tablets could be matched to transliterations row by row. The critique showed that this breaks:

  • Proto-Elamite has no ruled cells. Entries often start at the bottom of one column and carry on at the top of the next (Englund 2004).
  • Each line of the CDLI transliteration is one logical entry, not one physical line on the tablet (Born et al. 2025).
  • The reverse side is rotated, and older sign lists print some signs mirrored.

Solution: numerals are pressed in with a round stylus, while other signs are incised, and every entry ends in a numeral. Detecting numerals first turns one hard layout-parsing problem into many small alignment problems.

Core deliverable

Dataset

PE-Tokens v1: token-level boxes linked to CDLI tablet numbers, entries and sign names. There is a hand-annotated gold set of ~40–50 tablets. Only annotations and image references are released, never the museum images themselves.

Pipeline

Tablet-face extraction → separate detection for impressed numerals and incised signs → numeral-anchored segmentation → alignment inside each segment (DINOv2 features, CC-BY Dahl drawings as templates, position priors, rotation and mirroring handled) → EM self-training.

Collation flags

A ranked list of places where the image disagrees with the transliteration, starting with numerals: count or shape mismatches, and notations that are invalid in every number system.

Software engineering

A concordance and review viewer ("show every M288 on clay") that logs expert decisions, plus a tested transliteration parser, data version control, CI and a checklist for a licence-compliant release.

Optional extension (only if gate G3 passes)

A pre-registered audit of sign variants on real clay. Before seeing any results, fix a list of ≤30 sign pairs: Dahl's tilde variants, the merges Born 2023 proposed, and matched random controls. Test each pair with MMD on the image embeddings of gold or position-aligned tokens. Tokens aligned by template matching are excluded, because they bias the result toward signs that already look like the drawing. Also check whether an apparent variant just tracks individual tablets. Validate on scripts with known answers (Cypro-Greek, Linear B glyphs) and with planted merges. Results are reported as "visually distinct / indistinguishable / insufficient evidence", never as claims about sign identity.

Reporting rule: say "accuracy" only where ground truth exists. Findings about the script itself are "flagged" or "supported by visual evidence". The word "decipherment" appears nowhere.

Cut during review

  • Inferring meaning from numerals: already done in Born et al. 2025 §5. The "known" meanings used to evaluate it were themselves inferred from numerals, so the evaluation would be circular.
  • Testing proposed Proto-Elamite ↔ Linear Elamite sign matches: the matches were partly chosen because the signs look alike, so a visual test would pass by construction. Linear Elamite also has only ~45 inscriptions in a different genre, and Dahl 2023 disputes that the scripts are related at all.
  • Estimating the size of the sign inventory: the published spread of 287 to 1,623 signs comes from how scholars define a sign, which clustering can't settle.
  • Training on hand copies first: many transliterations were made from those copies, so agreement between them is partly guaranteed. Copies are useful for bootstrapping, but they are never evidence.

Draft abstract

Proto-Elamite, the writing system of Iran around 3100–2900 BCE, survives on roughly 1,600 clay tablets and remains undeciphered apart from its numerals. Recent computational studies have revealed structure in the script, but all of them work from scholars' transliterations or from a single idealised drawing per sign. None has looked at the signs as they were actually pressed and drawn into clay. This thesis builds the first image-grounded dataset of Proto-Elamite sign tokens. Numeral notations anchor each tablet face into entry-sized segments, which are then aligned to the Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative's transliterations under weak supervision. On top of this alignment we build a tool that ranks places where the clay and the transliteration appear to disagree. We evaluate it against planted errors, against corrections specialists have already made across successive corpus versions, and in a blinded review by a Proto-Elamite specialist. We do not claim to decipher the script. We build infrastructure the field lacks: a way to see, query and check every attestation of a sign on the tablets themselves.

Prerequisites to secure first

  • A named Proto-Elamite expert for ~8–10 h, e.g. Kelley, Monroe or Dahl. Contact the SFU group (Born, Sarkar) early: they are the natural advisers and also the main scooping risk.
  • Written CDLI confirmation (and museum confirmation if CDLI requires it) for bulk research download and releasing annotations keyed to CDLI tablet numbers. Ask whether higher-resolution or RTI images are available.
  • Working CDLI API access. The GitHub dump hasn't been updated since August 2022.
  • The Born 2025 numeral test set; library access to Englund 2004, Dahl 2019, Kelley 2026 and Monroe et al. 2025 (paywalled; check for overlapping work).
  • Supervisor sign-off that decipherment is out of scope.
Part II · Historical cursive transcription

Verify, Don't Rewrite

Image-checked, risk-certified transcription of historical handwriting

Thesis in one sentence

A vision-language model proposes the reading, an independent handwriting recogniser checks every disputed word against the image, and a per-collection statistical calibration decides which words can be released without review, with a certified bound on their error rate. Everything else is flagged.

The trap is real, and it's measurable

Evidence from 2025–26 backs the pitch's core claim. Correcting transcriptions with an LLM often makes them worse (Crosilla 2025; Levchenko 2025). ICDAR 2026 HIPE-OCRepair calls over-correction a "recurring challenge". WildHandBench finds that 63–91% of model errors are driven by language expectations, against 49% for humans.

Frontier models are also very good. Humphries reports ~1.7% CER for Gemini 3 on English, where ~61% of the remaining errors are silent spelling "standardisation". So the trap lives mainly in non-English, abbreviated, damaged material, names and numbers. The thesis targets exactly those, rather than trying to beat Gemini on English CER.

Reframing "near-zero error"

Drop: near-zero error at page level. It isn't achievable on messy cursive, and reviewers would reject the claim.

Replace with: near-zero unflagged error, certified per collection, plus no silent normalisation. Every word is either auto-accepted (bounded error), flagged (with alternatives and image evidence) or marked illegible. Normalised text goes in a separate, labelled layer.

"On collection X, calibrated with 400 random lines, N% of words were released; with 90% confidence the error among released words is ≤ 2%. Under an unseen hand the bound was violated in V% of resamples."

Core deliverable

1 · Propose

An open local VLM (CHURRO 3B, EMNLP 2025, 15.5× cheaper than Gemini 2.5 Pro) as the main model, with one commercial model on capped, cached subsets. A PyLaia CTC recogniser, fine-tuned per collection, produces an N-best list and posteriors.

2 · Verify

Align the VLM and CTC readings. For each disputed word, a small learned gate combines: the length-normalised visual support ratio from forced alignment, membership of the N-best list, agreement between the two models, consistency across image views, period-LM vs modern-LM surprisal, and token class. Names, numbers and dates get stricter thresholds.

3 · Certify

A threshold is calibrated on one random line from each of 300–500 random pages, which makes calibration lines independent by design. Conformal risk control bounds the share of all words that are accepted and wrong; Learn-then-Test bounds the error rate among accepted words.

4 · Deliver

PAGE-XML with per-word confidence, alternatives and provenance; a review screen showing only flagged spans, with timing logs; cost per page; and HalluTrap-HTR, a probe suite with a pip-installable, CI-tested scoring package.

How many calibration words the guarantee needs

Exact one-sided binomial test, counting accepted words only. The takeaway: a 1% target is cheap to certify only when coverage is sacrificed. A realistic target is α = 1–2% with 300–500 random lines per collection. For names and numbers the rate is reported empirically, not certified, because there aren't enough of them.

Optional extension (decided at G4): do hallucinations break archival search?

Compare rare-entity search over four indexes: keyword relevance computed from the CTC model (standing in for the probabilistic indexing archives already run: HIMANIS, Carabela, PARES), raw VLM transcripts, gated output, and gated output with each word's calibrated alternatives also indexed. Compare at matched index size. Build lexicons from training documents only, and report out-of-vocabulary queries separately. Real archives already run probabilistic search, yet VLM papers report only CER and WER.

Cut during review

  • Token-level LLM decoding constrained to the recogniser's candidates: Athar (2026) found that choosing among a recogniser's top-25 candidates recovered <4% of the possible gain. Engineering cost is high for an uncertain payoff. It comes back only if M3 shows an oracle gap of ≥1–2 WER points.
  • Per-class "≤0.1% error on names" guarantees: that would need ~3,000 calibration names, and 300 lines hold ~150–300.
  • Layout analysis research, active learning, a phone-capture app, a real user study: too much for the timeline. Phone capture survives as one robustness slice, and review time is simulated unless ethics approval arrives early.
  • "First provenance audit trail" as a novelty claim: Athar already exports auditable PAGE-XML. It remains an engineering deliverable.

Closest prior work, and where the novelty sits

Each ingredient has prior art: Athar (review with bounded alternatives, Arabic manuscripts), GRC (abstention for VLM OCR via cross-view agreement; scene text, empirical, not conformal), WildHandBench (prior-driven error metric; modern handwriting), OmniHandwritingOCR ("plausible but visually unsupported corrections"). No paper the agents checked combines them on historical handwriting with a certified guarantee and shift measurement. Claim the combination, and avoid "first X" for any single component.

Draft abstract

Vision-language models now transcribe historical handwriting well, but on archival material they make the most dangerous kind of error. They produce fluent, plausible text that is not on the page: modernised spellings, "corrected" names, altered numbers. Character error rates hide these errors, and search engines spread them. We present an open pipeline in which a VLM proposes and an independent, visually grounded recogniser checks every disputed word by forced alignment. A per-collection calibration on a few hundred random lines then decides which words are released with a certified error bound, and flags the rest with image evidence and alternatives. We also release HalluTrap-HTR, a benchmark that separates the errors a corrector introduces from those it fixes, with slices for rare names, numbers, archaic spellings, abbreviations and controlled illegibility. On Early Modern German, Dutch and English collections we report how much of each archive can be released with certified low error, how quickly the guarantee breaks for unseen hands, and what it costs per page. The goal is not error-free transcription. It is to make every remaining error visible.
Recommendation

Which to pursue

Default to Part II (Verify, Don't Rewrite) unless you can clear Part I's week-3 gate. For an Advanced Software Engineering project it is the safer and stronger fit:

  • The data is open.
  • Every headline metric has real ground truth.
  • The software-engineering deliverables are central, not bolted on.
  • Every gate failure still leaves a complete thesis (a benchmark plus a systematic comparison).

Part I has the higher ceiling of prestige, but also higher dependencies. It stands or falls on things you can't control from a keyboard: a Proto-Elamite expert's time, CDLI's written permission, and whether tablet photos are good enough. Nearly all of its ground truth also depends on expert review.

A cheap way to decide: spend the next two weeks on Part I's gate-0 checks while starting Part II's baselines, since the M1 work for Part II is useful either way.

  1. Email CDLI about image and annotation permissions.
  2. Contact one Proto-Elamite scholar.
  3. Count tablets with usable photos via the CDLI API.

If all three come back positive, Part I is a genuinely distinctive thesis. Otherwise, go with Part II.

Before citing anything

The agents checked most key sources at abstract level and marked each one read, abstract-only or unverified in their notes. Several 2026 arXiv preprints are recent and unreviewed. A few figures, such as Loghi's reported accuracy, Humphries' cost numbers and some dataset licences, came from search snippets only. Read the full papers before they go into a formal proposal. Re-run the novelty search at M1 and M5; both fields are moving monthly.
